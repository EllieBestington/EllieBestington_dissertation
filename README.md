### Research Title: Complex belowground responses to episodic warming in the Arctic tundra

#### Repository Structure
Repository includes the datasets, figures, R scripts, statistical analysis scripts used for my undergraduate dissertation thesis.

#### Abstract 
The Arctic is warming four times faster the global average, and tundra plant communities are shifting towards increased shrub dominance. However, projecting these trajectories requires understanding how climate variability influences competitive dynamics between plant functional groups. Despite representing the majority of tundra plant biomass, the belowground response remains critically understudied.. Whilst warming magnitude correlates with vegetation change at broad scales, substantial heterogeneity exists across sites experiencing similar temperature increases. This suggests that the temporal structure of warming, i.e. heat event frequency, duration and magnitude, may prove more important than average conditions alone. Here, we analysed whether these heatwave characteristics influence belowground competitive strategies between shallow-rooting shrubs and deep-rooting graminoids through depth-dependent soil thermal stratification. We capitalised on a natural experiment at Qikiqtaruk Herschel Island, Yukon, where 2023 (episodic, multi-day heatwaves) and 2024 (gradual sustained warming) provided contrasting thermal regimes. Through combination of multi-decadal active layer monitoring, with laboratory experiments to show thermal stratification, we quantified root biomass response across functional groups and soil depth. We found episodic heatwaves produced significantly deeper active layer thaw (53.1 cm verses 37.8 cm averages) and created pronounced vertical thermal gradients (3.8-fold difference between surface and deep soils). However, functional group root biomass responses proved more complex than thermal stratification alone predicted. Unexpected patterns revealed potential interactions between thermal regime, physical disturbance and moisture availability, rather than isolated temperature effects. These findings emphasise the complexity of belowground tundra responses, highlighting that multi-factor interactions are essential when projecting Arctic vegetation response under future climate variability.
